Job Market Paper

Competitive Persuasion with a Noisy Judge
Latest version (PDF) SSRN

Can a firm that must select one of two competing agents benefit from judging their evidence with error, even though the error sometimes leads her to select a bad agent over a good one? I show that she can when the agents design their evidence themselves: noise weakens each agent's incentive to conceal, because a narrow lead over his rival no longer guarantees selection. A moderately noisy judge outperforms a perfectly accurate one unless agents are very likely to be bad, and attains the first best once they are more likely good than bad. For a broad class of noise distributions, the equilibrium is unique and simple: each agent concedes that he is bad with some probability and otherwise sends a single pooled signal. Within this class, the firm is always better off facing stronger agents, even though stronger agents may disclose less. Finally, the only selection rule that can deliver the first best for every prior leaves agents indifferent among all disclosures, whereas a firm that knows only whether the pool is strong or weak can make full disclosure the unique equilibrium.

Strategic Broadcasting of a Championship: Slicing the Matches Between TV Channels
with Dmitry Dagaev. International Journal of Sport Finance, 2026, 21(2), 101–115.
Journal
Abstract

Consider a company that owns two sports TV channels: a popular free-to-air channel and a narrow-profile pay one. The company bought the broadcasting rights for a round-robin championship. When developing the broadcasting strategy, the company’s management faces the trade-off between inducing viewers to subscribe to the pay-TV channel and getting revenues from advertisements on the free-to-air channel. Motivated by an anecdotal case of a significant drop in the percentage of broadcasts of best teams among all broadcasts on a free-to-air channel, we propose a theoretical model of broadcasting the championship. We demonstrate that a wide range of strategies can be optimal depending on the market parameters. Whereas the intermediate strategy of broadcasting just weak matches (instead of broadcasting top matches or broadcasting nothing) on the free-to-air channel seems puzzling, we show formally that it can be fully rational for some market configurations. Therefore, we rationalize the puzzling strategy used by the company’s management in the motivational example.

Working Papers

Demand, Cost and Information Linkages in Markets with Decentralized Information
SSRN
Abstract

How do linkages between markets affect the way producers competing in supply schedules use dispersed information about their costs? On their own, both the demand and the cost linkage let the error in one market's public information distort output in the other, and raise welfare losses. A demand linkage acts only through prices: it raises the efficient weight on private information about the own cost, toward the market's excessive weight, and lowers the share of the value of cost information that is lost. A cost linkage also acts through marginal costs: it lowers this efficient weight and raises this share. The two effects cancel when demand and cost parameters are proportional: what matters is the misalignment of the two linkages, not their strength. In symmetric markets, correlated public information moves the response to the other market against both structural linkages and can offset either of them. When firms learn from prices, the demand and the cost linkage also change what the prices reveal and make them correlated public signals. The efficient welfare loss still falls as information becomes more precise, but the weights on private information about the own cost can move in either direction as either linkage strengthens.

The Stability of Expert Panels in Persuasion Games
Undergraduate thesis
SSRN
Abstract

Can adding an expert to a panel make a decision-maker worse off, even though the expert can only reveal information? I study a persuasion game in which a decision-maker consults a panel of experts who have state-independent agendas, may be uninformed, and can only disclose or conceal verifiable evidence. A new expert reveals some states that were concealed, but he also shifts the decision-maker's default action, the action she takes when no expert reports, and the other experts adjust what they reveal. I show that a panel of sufficiently extreme experts is stable: no additional expert, whatever his agenda and quality, makes the decision-maker worse off. A panel of moderate experts is generically unstable: for every quality level, some expert close to the default action shifts it so that an incumbent stops revealing states far from it. With commitment to the default action every panel is stable, and extreme panels behave as if the decision-maker could commit. Polarized committees can therefore be expanded safely, while adding a centrist to a moderate committee can backfire.
